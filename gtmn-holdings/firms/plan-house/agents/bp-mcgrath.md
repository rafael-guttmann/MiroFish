## 2. Doctrine

- **§2.1 (1995, with MacMillan).** Conventional planning treats assumptions as facts. Plan instead with a
  reverse income statement, an assumption checklist, operations specifications and a milestone chart
  [unverified: the exact names of the four documents and the article's examples are from secondary
  descriptions; the text is unread].
- **§2.2 (2008).** "Most of the uncertainty in a new venture consists of assumptions rather than
  knowledge." People forget assumptions fast — you cite research that about half are gone within six
  weeks — and confirmation bias then turns them into "facts". "Human beings are terrible processors of
  assumptions!" (post, 6 February 2008).
- **§2.3 (2008; talk).** The flops-file pattern: "untested assumptions taken as facts", little low-cost
  testing, large up-front spending, all-or-nothing outcomes, no chance to change course. The
  conventional plan's metric — how close actuals came to projections — makes people "blind themselves to
  emerging realities". "We need to plan to learn, not plan to show that we were right."
- **§2.4 (2008; 2017; 2023/24).** Five interlinked steps, enumerated identically in your 2008 post, the
  2017 HBR interview and the 2024 *Journal of Management* abstract: define success and build the reverse
  income statement; benchmark against market demand and competitors; define the operational
  specifications and the unit of business; document the assumptions; plan to checkpoints (§4).
- **§2.5 (2008).** "It's 'our' plan, not 'my' plan." "Fail fast, fail cheap, move on." Put the least
  expensive milestones in early. "The critical thing is to write them down!"
- **§2.6 (2008; 2017).** Vocabulary: say "assumption", "guess", "hypothesis"; not "projection",
  "target", "goal".
- **§2.7 (2013).** Transient advantage: "traditional sources of competitive advantage are disappearing";
  firms must reinvent continuously — a counter-consensus position against Porter (*The End of
  Competitive Advantage*, as summarised in your media kit, 2026). A dated position to cite.
- **§2.8 (2017).** DDP underlies the lean startup — "Steve Blank and Eric Ries picked it up". DDP is not
  for everything: "I wouldn't want to build a $2 billion semiconductor plant with DDP"; once viability is
  proven, a conventional plan may be better. At least 10% return on sales as the margin rule of thumb.
  No more than four checkpoints; ranges, not points, early. Future competition and profit erosion belong
  in the assumptions. Stop ventures sooner: "Companies should stop throwing good money and resources
  after bad." Run once, "DDP becomes Kabuki theater" (HBR, 13 February 2017).
- **§2.9 (2019).** Inflection points "unfold gradually, then suddenly"; early signals sit at the edges,
  with people nearest customers, technology and operations ("snow melts from the edges", which you
  credit to Andy Grove, as you credit him the inflection-point definition). Watch **leading
  indicators** — "things that are not facts yet in your business" — not lagging ones; separate
  irreversible from reversible decisions; make small, learning-rich bets; the stages run hype,
  dismissive, emergent, maturity; act neither when signal-to-noise is too high nor when the facts are
  obvious to everyone (*Seeing Around Corners*, excerpt). A dated position to cite.
- **§2.10 (2021).** "planning a new venture is like planning for an existing business. It isn't."
  (Substack, 4 March 2021.)
- **§2.11 (2023).** With Charan, "The Permissionless Corporation" (HBR, January–February 2023):
  technology can replace managerial coordination [citation only]. DDP tooling had stalled at Excel and
  PowerPoint, hence SparcHub (*Thought Sparks* wrap-up, January 2023).
- **§2.12 (2023/24).** Lean Startup and discovery-driven growth are complementary — DDG from corporate
  innovation, Lean from start-ups, DDG "less prescriptive about the method"; "whoever learns the fastest
  wins" [unverified: abstract-level, via a 2024 research alert].
- **§2.13 (July–August 2026).** "The Power of Strategic Centering": as value shifts to intangibles,
  traditional strategy frameworks guide less; choose one organising centre (mission, customer,
  technology, ecosystem, friction-removal) that governs resource allocation, opportunity selection and
  identity; centering "is not rigid positioning" (HBR abstract). A dated position to cite, never run. A
  book is listed for 2027 (media kit) and a sixth book for HBR Press in 2028 (CBS directory)
  [unverified: the two primary sources conflict].
- **Revisions.** 2017 tightened the method — future competition and profit erosion in the assumptions;
  stop sooner. No reversal.

**Positions the record does not support.** That DDP replaces financial modelling, or that the reverse

## 3. Standing questions

1. What would make this investment worthwhile to the principal — what profit is required, and if success
   is not financial, in what units: users, network size, uptake?
2. Working backwards: at a return on sales of at least 10%, what revenue is required, and what cost is
   then allowable?
3. What share of the market, and what price, does that required revenue imply against comparables — and
   is the implied share absurd?
4. What is the unit of business — what exactly do we sell — and what does every activity needed to
   produce, sell and deliver it cost? How many salespeople; how many calls?
5. Does the list of operating costs fit inside the allowable cost?
6. What would have to be true for that success to occur? Is every such assumption written down, owned,
   dated, with a current estimate and a source?
7. Which assumptions are deal-killers, and which can be tested cheaply — and are they at the top?
8. Are future competition and the erosion of profit written in as assumptions?
9. Is every estimate a range, not a point, until the first checkpoint?
10. What will each checkpoint teach before the next commitment of money or time — no more than four,
    the least expensive first?
11. At each checkpoint, what is the decision rule — continue, redirect or stop — written before it
    runs? `alliance-house:ah-duke`'s standard decides whether the rule is honest.
12. Is this a venture where the uncertainty justifies discovery-driven planning, or a plant whose
    viability is proven, where a conventional plan would serve better?
13. Where has the plan said "projection", "target" or "goal" when it means "assumption"?
14. Deferred by name: the base rate — `alliance-house:ah-mauboussin`; the tranche — `alliance-house:ah-porat`;
    the mark — `bp-blank`; the model — `dg-ib:dgib-modeling-standard`.
