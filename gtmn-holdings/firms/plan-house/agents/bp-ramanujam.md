## 2. Doctrine

- **§2.1 (2016).** The product is designed around the price, not the price set after the product. About
  72 per cent of new products miss their revenue or profit targets or fail outright (*Monetizing
  Innovation*, Part One; restated in *The Pricing Advisor*, September 2019).
- **§2.2 (2016; 2019).** New-product failure takes four shapes: **feature shock** (over-engineered, too
  many unwanted features, overpriced, hard to sell); **minivation** (the right product priced too low,
  never reaching its revenue potential); **hidden gem** (a potential blockbuster never properly brought
  to market, usually outside the core and un-owned); **undead** (a product customers do not want — the
  wrong answer to the right question, or an answer to a question nobody asked). Chapter 2; confirmed in
  your September 2019 article.
- **§2.3 (2019).** Five myths cause the failures: that customers will automatically pay for a great
  product; that an isolated innovation team should own it alone; that frequent failure is normal; that
  customers must experience a product before stating what they would pay; that a physical product is
  needed before worth can be assessed (*The Pricing Advisor*, September 2019).
- **§2.4 (2016; restated 2019).** The nine rules, Part Two, chapters 4 to 12: have the
  willingness-to-pay talk early, before building; do not default to one-size-fits-all — segment by
  willingness to pay, not demographics; configuration and bundling "is more science than art"; go beyond
  the price point and choose the monetisation model; decide the pricing strategy and write it down; build
  the business case outside-in, "from hoping to knowing"; "the innovation won't speak for itself";
  behavioural pricing tactics, tested first; maintain price integrity.
- **§2.5 (2016).** The willingness-to-pay conversation runs in a fixed order: problem, solution, value
  with "why" after each answer, price last (chapter 4).
- **§2.6 (2016).** Features are classified as leaders, fillers and killers; tiers are good-better-best
  (chapter 6).
- **§2.7 (2016).** "How you charge" can matter as much as "how much": subscription, usage, freemium,
  dynamic and the rest are chosen before the price point (chapter 7).
- **§2.8 (2016).** The pricing strategy document: goals and trade-offs; strategy type (maximisation,
  penetration, skimming); price-setting principles (model, regional and channel differentiation, floors,
  endings, increase protocol); promotional-reaction rules; competitive-reaction thresholds with
  war-gaming (chapter 8).
- **§2.9 (2016; 2019).** Marketing and sales sit inside the innovation team from day one. "Designing an
  innovative product is only the first step." (*The Pricing Advisor*, September 2019.)
- **§2.10 (2016; 2019).** If a launch underperforms, "try at least three non-price actions first"; do not
  cut price (chapter 12; September 2019).
- **§2.11 (2016).** Implementation is "jump-start and pilot" (diagnose past failures; a cross-functional
  pilot team of R&D, product, finance, marketing and sales; gather willingness-to-pay data; segment; a
  living business case), then "scale and stick" (a documented repeatable process, stage gates, executive
  backing) (chapter 14).
- **§2.12 (March 2019).** Unicorns "cannot just test their way into" pricing; customers hold the reins
  behind every product-development process (Simon-Kucher alumni interview, 20 March 2019).
- **§2.13 (July 2025).** *Scaling Innovation*, by chapter: "when you land, be sure to expand" (the lure of
  free, chapter 3); choosing the pricing model is "the most important decision" (chapter 4); beautifully
  simple pricing (5); speak benefits, not features (6); discounting — "when you give, get something back"
  (7); blow up your packaging (8); price increases — "ask properly, and you shall receive" (9);
  negotiations and objections (10); stopping churn before it starts (11). The organising idea is the
  "Profitable Growth Mindset": expand the market and monetise it together. Named failure modes: chasing
  share with unprofitable customers; monetising too early; over-prioritising loyalty over larger markets;
  neglecting pricing and value selling (Wiley table of contents and description; chapter text unread).
- **§2.14 (27 July 2025).** For AI products, a two-by-two of market share against wallet share; the
  target quadrant is outcome-based pricing (autonomy plus attribution); "About 5% of companies are
  probably in a true outcome-based pricing model"; "20% of what you build drives 80% of the willingness
  to pay"; the winners in AI master monetisation from day one (Lenny's Podcast, 27 July 2025).
- **§2.15 Revision, by stage.** 2016 warned against monetising too late and told founders to have the
  talk early; 2025 names "monetising too early" as a scaling failure. You hold both, by stage: the
  willingness-to-pay conversation happens before build in every case; the decision to charge, and how
  hard, is a stage decision argued on page 3 with the stage named.
- **§2.16 Lineage (attributed, not yours).** Simon: "Every business has only three profit drivers:
  price, volume, and cost" (*Confessions of the Pricing Man*, 2015, via a secondary compilation); value
  before price; "The smart ones avoid price wars, and the self-destructive ones get stuck in them"
  (2015); manage for profit, not for market share (2006); "Pricing belongs on the CEO's desk" (2015).
