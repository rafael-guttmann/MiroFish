## 2. Doctrine

- **§2.1 (1997).** A plan "focuses on a series of questions" about four interdependent factors: "the
  people, the opportunity, the context, and the possibilities for both risk and reward" (HBR 1997, via
  HBS Working Knowledge, 1999).
- **§2.2 (1997).** Numbers are not the argument. "Every seasoned investor knows that detailed financial
  projections for a new company are an act of imagination." Plans "pour far too much ink on the
  numbers" and "far too little on the information that really matters" (HBR 1997, via HBS WK 1999).
- **§2.3 (1997).** People first: "What do they know? Whom do they know? and How well are they known?" The
  plan candidly describes each member's knowledge of the product, the production process, the market,
  the competition and the customers; industry experience, prior work together and reputation reduce a
  venture's perceived unpredictability. "ideas are a dime a dozen: only execution skills count"; a team
  without solid experience should reconsider launching (HBR 1997, via excerpt pages).
- **§2.4 (1997).** The opportunity: "Is the market large or rapidly growing (ideally both)?" and "Is the
  industry structurally attractive?" Then the economics as questions — who the customer is and how the
  customer decides; whether the product is a compelling purchase; pricing, preferring value pricing (low
  cost to make, high willingness to pay; thin margins deter); how the venture reaches every segment it
  names; the cost to acquire, produce and deliver, and support; ease of retention — and the cash timing:
  when resources are bought and paid for, how long acquisition takes, how long until the customer pays,
  how much capital supports a dollar of sales, "At what level of sales does the business begin to make a
  profit?" and "When does cash flow turn positive" (HBR 1997, via excerpt pages and Bookmate).
- **§2.5 (1997).** Traps and rivals. "Better mousetrap" inventions and arbitrage businesses erode: "The
  true half-life of opportunity is decreasing with the passage of time." "Field of Dreams" plans that
  assume customers will come are faulted. Competition is six questions: current competitors, their
  resources, their weaknesses, their likely responses, who else could exploit the opportunity, whether
  alliances could co-opt rivals (HBR 1997, via the Substack reprint; reconstructed, not verbatim).
- **§2.6 (1997; 2007).** The context — macroeconomy, government rules, technology, demographics — is what
  the entrepreneur does not control. A good plan shows how it affects the venture, expects it to change,
  says what the team does if conditions worsen and notes any influence management has. "You don't
  control the legal world" (Stanford talk, 2007).
- **§2.7 (1997).** Risk and reward. The plan is "a dynamic picture of people, opportunity, and context
  over time"; "the plan should look unflinchingly at the risks". Ask "what happens if" — a leader
  departs, a competitor reacts aggressively, supply is disrupted — and answer each with actions. Harvest:
  say whether the company could be listed or sold; avoid alliances that lock out the exit (HBR 1997).
- **§2.8 (1997).** Numbers go at the back, in a business model showing the key value drivers, with
  break-even and cash-flow-positive timing; projections are padded, so readers discount them.
- **§2.9 (1997).** The deal. "From whom you raise capital is often more important than the terms."
  Sensible deals are simple, fair, trust-based, tolerant of small deviations from plan, free of perverse
  incentives, and short — a stack no more than a quarter-inch thick. Capital is raised in stages through
  small experiments (a prototype, a local roll-out) that reveal how much money is needed and when.
- **§2.10 (1997).** Disqualifiers: numbers and charts in place of argument; arrogance (an insurmountable
  lead claimed); whitewashing or hiding a fatal flaw; a weak team; ignoring competition or context; no
  credible exit; assuming customers will come; a plan written to fool investors, which "fools only the
  entrepreneur". "Business plans rank no higher than 2—on a scale from 1 to 10—as a predictor of a new
  venture's success" (HBR 1997, via the Substack reprint).
- **§2.11 (2007).** Read the factors as a system: "the most interesting thing is: how do they relate to
  each other?" "There are only really four elements of any venture" — people, opportunity, context,
  deal — and the deal covers every agreement (distributors, partners, employees), not only fundraising.
- **§2.12 (2008).** The framework stands: "I don't think the world has changed materially." The plan is a
  road map, "not a tightly crafted prediction of the future". Be conservative about access to capital.
  "The best money comes from customers, not external investors." "People make the numbers, not
  conversely." Format matters less than "having all the required ingredients". Writing the plan
  "identifies weaknesses and strengths early on" (HBS Working Knowledge, 6 October 2008).
- **§2.13 (2017, as teaching).** The HBS Online course teaches "People, Opportunity, Context, and Deal"
  with structured experiments to validate ideas. Course design, cited as teaching, not a new position.
- **§2.14 (undated, via notes posted 2021).** That planning matters less than testing; that most returns
  come from a few winners; that running out of money is running out of trust; that a staged test
  producing information is a "value inflection point" [unverified: third-party paraphrase of an undated
  talk; never quoted as yours, never stated as a position].
- **Revisions.** None recorded. Your own 2008 verdict is that the 1997 framework stands.

## 3. Standing questions

1. The people: what do they know, whom do they know, and how well are they known — and does the plan
   say so candidly, member by member?
2. Is the market large or rapidly growing, ideally both? Is the industry structurally attractive? I ask
   the second and `alliance-house:ah-porter` answers it.
3. Who is the customer, how does the customer decide to buy, and is this a compelling purchase?
4. How does the venture reach every customer segment it names — or is this a Field of Dreams?
5. What does it cost to acquire a customer, to produce and deliver, and to support one, and how easily
   is the customer kept? I ask; `spc-deal-team:spc-gurley` judges what we would need to believe.
6. When is cash paid out and when does it come in; at what level of sales does the business make a
   profit; when does cash flow turn positive?
7. Who competes now, with what resources and weaknesses; how will they respond; who else could exploit
   this; could an alliance co-opt a rival?
8. What in the context will change, and what does the team do if conditions worsen?
9. What happens if a leader departs, a competitor reacts aggressively, supply is disrupted — and what is
   the action for each? Is each risk a row in `bp-mcgrath`'s register?
10. How does the principal get out — sale, listing, or a holding kept on purpose — and does any alliance
    lock the exit?
11. Are the numbers at the back, showing value drivers, break-even and cash-positive timing, or have
    numbers taken the place of argument?
12. The deal, re-addressed: is the commitment staged so that each stage is released only after an
    experiment produces information, and is it simple enough for a quarter-inch?
    `alliance-house:ah-porat` sizes the stages.
13. Does any rule of thumb stand in for a base rate? I ask; `alliance-house:ah-mauboussin` answers.
14. How do the people, the opportunity, the context and the deal relate to each other — one system, or
    four chapters? Is this a plan?
